The World Is Traveling Again. Why Is the U.S. Capturing Less International Demand?

The return of international travel exposes a competitiveness question for the United States: scale and recognition remain enormous, but share, entry friction and the ability to turn attention into value matter more than before.

Lipe CamanzanoPublished Sep 5, 20269 min read
LTH reading · September 2026. The question is not whether the United States remains one of the world’s largest tourism markets. It is why international inbound recovery remains below the potential of such extensive infrastructure, supply and destination appeal.

International tourism is growing again, but recovery is not distributed evenly. The U.S. contrast is particularly useful for destination economics: scale and awareness do not guarantee automatic capture of global demand.

Market size can hide the problem

U.S. Travel Association forecasts 70.6 million international visitors in 2026, up 3.4% from 2025. Even so, the country is not expected to recover its 2019 volume of 79 million visitors until 2029. In real terms, 2026 inbound spending remains 18% below 2019.

The useful question therefore changes. Instead of simply asking whether tourism is back, destinations need to ask what share of global recovery they are capturing — and at what value.

Competitiveness also happens before boarding

Price, exchange rate and connectivity matter, but travelers also evaluate friction: visa time and cost, predictability of entry, international perception, clarity of the journey and whether they expect to feel welcome. U.S. Travel itself identifies visas, wait times and sentiment in source markets as risks to inbound performance.

Mega-events buy attention; they do not guarantee repeat demand

The 2026 World Cup expanded U.S. exposure and helped support the year’s recovery. But an event is an acquisition of attention. Destination competitiveness requires converting the peak into relationships, territorial dispersal, length of stay, spending and intent to return.

The advantage shifts toward reducing friction and increasing value

Mature destinations do not compete only on awareness. They compete on the quality of conversion between desire and trip. Public policy, airports, distribution, product, communication and the arrival experience need to work as one system.

A destination can remain enormous and still lose share in the next wave of growth.

What this means for the industry

For DMOs, airlines, hotels and distributors, volume alone is not the decisive metric. More useful indicators include share of available demand, spend per visitor, ease of access, repeat behavior and the ability to turn events and awareness into durable economic value.
