Travelers Don’t Just Want Better Places to Visit. They Want to Experience What Living There Feels Like

Quality of life, mobility, safety, nature and everyday routines are entering the tourism value proposition. For longer-stay travelers, a destination is increasingly judged by what it feels like to live there, not only to visit.

Lipe CamanzanoPublished Sep 5, 20268 min read
LTH reading · September 2026. As the boundary between visiting, working and temporarily living in a place becomes more porous, attributes traditionally used to attract residents also begin to influence travel choice.

Zurich was identified in 2026 as one of the world’s most attractive cities to live in, with quality of life, safety, health, income and stability among its strengths. Rankings like this appear to belong to relocation. Increasingly, however, they also say something about tourism.

Travelers are testing lifestyles

Flexcation, remote work, longer stays and repeat travel make it possible to experience a city as a temporary resident. The neighborhood café, supermarket, public transport, park, sense of safety and ability to work well begin to compete with traditional attractions.

Sightseeing does not disappear. A new layer of value is added: what would my life feel like here?

Liveability can become destination equity

Cities known for organization, mobility, nature, education, food and urban quality accumulate attributes that can influence both residence and travel. The same bike lane improving residents’ lives improves the visitor experience. The same is true of parks, safety and connectivity.

The product changes when the stay becomes longer

Longer stays create demand for kitchen, laundry, workspace, gym, convenience, neighborhood access and coherent pricing. Hotels, serviced apartments and short-term rentals increasingly compete in a space between hospitality and temporary living.

When a traveler stays longer, the city stops being scenery and becomes routine.

Quality of life is also communication

Many destinations still promote mainly monuments and landscapes. For long-stay audiences, creators, executives in blended travel and mobile professionals, showing how a place is lived can be more persuasive: walking, eating, working, meeting people, exercising and reaching nature.

There is an important limit

Turning liveability into a tourism argument requires care. Additional demand can place pressure on housing, services and neighborhoods. The same asset attracting visitors can be degraded by poorly managed growth. Lifestyle tourism therefore needs to connect with capacity, regulation and local benefit.

What this means for destinations

DMOs can broaden their definition of competitiveness by incorporating indicators usually associated with cities to live in: mobility, safety, digital connectivity, access to nature, relative cost, services and neighborhood experience. The next-generation tourism destination may also be a destination for temporary life.
