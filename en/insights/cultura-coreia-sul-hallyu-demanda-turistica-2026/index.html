When Culture Becomes Tourism: What South Korea Teaches About Turning Hallyu Into Demand

K-pop, film, series, food, beauty and fashion now work together as an infrastructure of desire for South Korea. Hallyu shows how cultural influence can build travel demand long before a destination campaign begins.

Lipe CamanzanoPublished Sep 5, 20269 min read
LTH reading · September 2026. The Korean lesson is not “use celebrities to promote tourism.” It is to build a cultural ecosystem capable of generating curiosity, familiarity and concrete reasons to travel.

Hallyu — the Korean cultural wave — shows how culture can move beyond promotion and become demand infrastructure. K-pop, K-dramas, film, food, beauty, fashion and gaming create multiple entry points into the same destination.

Desire begins long before the tourism campaign

A person may first encounter Seoul through a series, learn K-pop choreography, buy Korean beauty products, try kimchi at home and only later consider a trip. By the time tourism enters the conversation, the destination brand may already have been built through years of cultural exposure.

Travel + Leisure describes Seoul as a hub for food, art, film, television, literature, beauty and fashion, recognizing that much of this energy is connected with the Korean Wave. By 2026, itineraries inspired by entertainment phenomena were already converting pop narratives into commercial tourism products.

Cultural influence works because it is distributed

There is no single channel. Music, streaming, social platforms, cinema, creators, fandoms and events reinforce one another. That reduces dependence on conventional destination-marketing campaigns and creates repeated contact with the destination in the traveler’s everyday life.

The tourism product must keep up with the imagination

The mistake would be to stop at photo spots. When culture generates demand, the destination needs experiences that deepen knowledge: neighborhoods, food, performances, workshops, fashion, history, heritage and encounters with creative communities.

Korea’s Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism works explicitly across these categories. In 2025, My K-Festa brought together K-pop, fashion, K-food and lifestyle and received about 17,000 international tourists among more than 46,000 participants.

Soft power becomes tourism when cultural influence meets product, distribution and experience on the ground.

Seoul is layered destination branding

The city does not depend on one icon. Historic heritage, design, technology, cafés, nightlife, gastronomy and pop culture coexist. Different travelers therefore find different reasons to visit the same place — and the destination appears more frequently in the everyday lives of future visitors.

What other destinations can learn

The lesson is not to copy K-pop. It is to identify authentic cultural assets, invest in creative ecosystems, make international distribution easier and convert attention into bookable experiences. Culture should not be treated as campaign decoration; it can function as an economic engine.
