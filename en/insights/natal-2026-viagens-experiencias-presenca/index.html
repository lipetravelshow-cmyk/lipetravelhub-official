Christmas 2026: The Most Valuable Gift May Be a Shared Experience

Christmas travel is more than a seasonal spike in rates and occupancy. Shared experiences, presence and memory are gaining ground in how families allocate both attention and travel spending.

Lipe CamanzanoPublished Sep 4, 20269 min read
New editorial reading · September 2026. This edition uses Christmas to discuss a broader shift: families increasingly value shared experiences, roots and quality time as part of what they consider a “gift.”

Christmas has always been about presence. Travel is beginning to turn that into product.

For decades, the year-end season was treated mainly as demand: occupancy, rate, air capacity, packages and consumption. All remain relevant. But another layer is becoming more visible: travel itself has become part of the way families celebrate. In 2026, behavior research points to greater value being placed on shared experiences, multigenerational journeys and reconnection with traditions.

1. Experience is competing with the material gift

In the Brazilian cut of Hilton’s 2026 Trends Report, 80% of families say they value experiences more than material gifts. Commercially, that moves travel, accommodation and local experiences into the family’s emotional budget. A trip can be the gift. A special dinner can be the gift. A weekend involving three generations can be the gift.

2. The contemporary family needs more flexible formats

Families no longer travel only as parents and children. Grandparents travel with grandchildren; adults travel with older parents; extended families meet in an intermediate destination. Hospitality and destinations therefore need connecting rooms, accessibility, adaptable food, shared spaces, simple logistics and activities that work across ages.

3. Local tradition can be a real tourism asset

Lighting, Christmas markets, food, music, religious celebrations, crafts and local rituals can become much more than seasonal decoration. When authentic, they become content, product and a reason to travel. A destination does not need to imitate New York, Vienna or London. It needs to translate its own identity into the season.

4. The season requires less friction and more care

Year-end travel combines high demand with high emotional expectations. Small failures therefore become bigger: confusing service, poorly coordinated transfers, unclear schedules or overcrowded experiences can undermine the meaning of the trip. Technology should organize the journey, while people need to appear when care, improvisation and empathy make the difference.

5. Christmas also creates an “inheritourism” opportunity

Hilton reports growing interest in trips connected with family roots: more than half of global families plan travel to learn about their history. Year-end celebrations provide a natural context. Visiting a grandparents’ town, seeing relatives, cooking a family recipe or learning a regional tradition can turn travel into the transmission of memory.

6. The opportunity is to design the gathering

Hospitality brands cannot control the emotion of Christmas, but they can create better conditions for it: arrival, accommodation, table, activity, rest, accessibility and time together. The premium product may be luxury, but it can also simply be the absence of friction.

7. What remains after the decoration

The best seasonal strategy does not end on December 26. If the experience creates memory, it can support retention, reputation, organic content and return the following year. The challenge is to turn the emotional peak into a relationship without mechanizing what worked precisely because it felt human.

The 2026 insight: presence has acquired economic value

In a faster, more automated and screen-mediated world, shared time has become scarce. That helps explain the strength of family travel, celebrations and reconnection experiences. At Christmas, the traveler does not buy only a stay; they buy the possibility of living a memory together.
